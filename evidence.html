Technology Implementation

Two public-safety implementation case studies

IMPL-2007-ALPR-TARGET-BLUEImplementation case study

ALPR program | Target + Blue and Genetec AutoVu

From 2007–2010, Troy served as the Lakeville Police Department's ALPR program lead for a $40,000 Target + Blue corporate partnership grant supporting Genetec AutoVu. The work required coordination across Target Corporation, BCA CJIS, Genetec and Lakeville Information Technology.

Operational problem
Convert grant funding into a reliable field capability that could receive current license-plate hotlists and fit practical patrol and investigative workflows.
Stakeholders
Target Corporation's Target + Blue program, BCA CJIS, Genetec AutoVu, Lakeville Information Technology and agency end users.
Requirements
Reliable nightly hotlist synchronization, CJIS-aware data handling, municipal IT compatibility and a workflow usable in field operations.
Implementation
Collaborated on the corporate grant with a reserve officer who also worked in Target Assets Protection, then coordinated the agency-side program across funding, Genetec AutoVu, BCA CJIS, Lakeville IT and operational workflow.
Training / adoption
Supported field adoption by connecting system behavior and data availability to practical patrol and investigative use. This public summary does not claim formal Genetec trainer status or system-administrator authority.
Troubleshooting
Worked across BCA CJIS, Genetec and Lakeville IT to support reliable automatic hotlist transfers over Wi-Fi on the nightly update cycle.
Adoption result
The ALPR program operated from 2007–2010 with automated daily hotlist updates incorporated into the agency workflow.
Lesson learned
Public-safety technology succeeds when funding, data integration, municipal IT, vendor support and frontline workflow are solved together rather than treated as separate problems.

Source basis: verified career constants and technology inventory. No procurement authority beyond the documented program-lead and grant-collaboration role is implied.

IMPL-2016-ECU-CAPABILITY-BUILDImplementation case study

Electronic-crimes capability build | Cellebrite UFED and investigator enablement

During the 2016–2017 Electronic Crimes Unit assignment, Troy acquired and configured the unit's initial Cellebrite UFED capability and developed a structured investigator resource package for electronic-evidence work. The unit itself predated Troy's assignment; this case study describes his documented infrastructure and enablement contribution, not unit creation.

Operational problem
Investigators entering electronic-crimes work faced a learning curve spanning mobile-device evidence, legal process, provider requirements and escalation to specialized digital-forensics resources.
Stakeholders
Lakeville patrol and investigations personnel, the Electronic Crimes Unit, the Dakota County Electronic Crimes Task Force and external service-provider processes.
Requirements
An initial mobile-forensics acquisition capability plus repeatable guidance for administrative subpoenas, preservation requests, search warrants and ISP/ESP reference information.
Implementation
Acquired and configured the initial Cellebrite UFED capability, built the investigator training folder and served as a bridge between patrol/investigations and the DCECTF task force.
Training / adoption
Designed the resource package to reduce the learning curve for investigators new to electronic-crimes work. A later performance review identified Troy as a digital-forensics subject-matter expert and peer resource. See evidence excerpt.
Troubleshooting
Created a repeatable path for technical and legal-process questions to move from frontline investigators to documented guidance or specialized task-force support instead of being handled as isolated problems.
Adoption result
Converted recurring preservation, subpoena, warrant and service-provider questions into reusable investigator workflows and reference material.
Lesson learned
A technical tool is only part of implementation. Adoption improves when capability is paired with legal-process guidance, reusable documentation and a clear escalation path.

Source basis: verified career constants and private performance-review evidence. No claim is made that Troy founded the Electronic Crimes Unit or led the DCECTF.
